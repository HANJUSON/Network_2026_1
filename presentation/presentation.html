What is HFT — and why does 1 μs matter?

High-Frequency Trading is algorithmic, microsecond-scale automated trading. Orders are decided and submitted by machines, not humans.

  • ~50% of all US equity trading volume is HFT.
  • A 1 ms latency advantage is worth roughly tens of millions / year to a major bank.
  • Strategies live and die on who arrives first, not who is cleverest.
This is not a niche problem. It's a 50%-of-the-market problem.

01 · The Problem

Winner-Takes-All — tail latency beats mean

When a price moves, only the first arriving order takes the trade. Second place wins nothing.

  • A system with mean 100 μs but P99 of 200 ms is effectively unusable.
  • The mean lies. The tail is where the money lives — or evaporates.
  • Our target metric for the rest of the talk: P99 RTT under packet loss.
HFT lives or dies at the tail, not at the average.

Latency is not Gaussian. The right tail is exactly the regime HFT cares about.

01 · The Problem

dqueue is non-linear — that's the real danger

As traffic intensity ρ = La / R → 1, queuing delay does not grow linearly — it explodes.

  • Average delay is fine until utilization approaches capacity.
  • Then a single burst of loss / re-tx forces buffers to back up — and tail latency goes from microseconds to milliseconds or worse.
  • Flash Crash (2014): 10-year US Treasury fell 33–34 bp, then recovered, in ~10 minutes — a real-world demonstration of dqueue non-linearity.
Average is fine until ρ → 1. Then everything explodes.

02 · The Solution — Kernel Bypass

1 μs = Millions — attack dproc first

Boseok identified dproc as one of the two software-controlled delays. In a standard TCP/IP stack, almost all of dproc is spent inside the Linux kernel.

  • A single round-trip enters the kernel twice (RX + TX).
  • Each entry is 10–30 μs — 20–60 μs wasted per round-trip.
  • Top firms target tick-to-trade < 10 μs — impossible if the kernel is on the path.
The kernel is not slow because it's badly written. It's slow by design — for safety, fairness, and generality. HFT needs none of those.

02 · The Solution — Kernel Bypass

Why the Kernel Wastes Microseconds

① Interrupts

Every incoming packet raises an IRQ. Context switch + cache pollution → cost per packet, not per batch.

② Memory copies

Packet copied from NIC ring → kernel sk_buff → user buffer. Each copy is hundreds of nanoseconds at best.

③ Context switching

System calls (recv / send) cross the user/kernel boundary. Each crossing flushes registers and TLB.

④ Protocol processing

TCP state machine, congestion control, checksum, scheduling — written for generality, not for 1 μs targets.

If the kernel is the bottleneck, the answer is to skip it entirely. That is exactly what kernel-bypass technologies do.

Three Bypass Approaches — Performance vs Compatibility

OpenOnload

≈ 1–3 μs

User-space TCP/IP stack injected via LD_PRELOAD. No code change. Requires Solarflare NIC.

Best for: drop-in latency win.

DPDK

< 1 μs

Poll-mode driver. App owns the NIC directly. Full rewrite required. Huge pages, NUMA pinning, busy-poll cores.

Best for: greenfield ultra-low latency.

RDMA

≈ 1–2 μs

NIC writes directly to remote memory. Remote CPU not involved. Requires InfiniBand or RoCE fabric.

Best for: intra-datacenter.

The trade is always the same: the more of the kernel you bypass, the faster you go — and the more your code and hardware must change.

03 · Empirical Validation

Why? Linux RTO_min = 200 ms

TCP cannot detect a lost packet faster than its Retransmission Timeout. On Linux, the floor of that timeout is hardcoded:

// net/ipv4/tcp.c (Linux kernel) #define TCP_RTO_MIN ((unsigned)(HZ/5)) // = 200 ms

On a clean network, RTT is ~180 μs. But if a packet is lost, TCP waits at least 200 ms before even retrying.

TCP guarantees delivery — but its guarantee mechanism is the latency.

The mechanism, step by step

  1. Order packet sent.
  2. Lost by tc netem.
  3. Receiver never sees it → no ACK.
  4. Sender's RTO timer expires after ≥ 200 ms.
  5. Retransmit. ACK finally arrives.
  6. RTT for that one order: ~205 ms.
This is exactly Boseok's dqueue non-linear blowup — measured on our own machine.

UDP Trade-off — 8/1000 lost, but the tail stays flat

  • Under 1% loss, UDP loses 8 out of 1000 orders.
  • The other 992 arrive with the same latency as the no-loss baseline.
  • UDP doesn't try to recover. That refusal is its speed.
UDP refuses to wait. That refusal is its speed.
For HFT…Loss205 ms tail
Market data feed (price ticks) Next tick fixes it Trade opportunity gone
Order entry (sending a trade) Lost order = lost trade Tolerable (rarely happens)

The asymmetry, in plain terms

For a market data feed, missing one price tick is recoverable — the next update replaces it in microseconds.

For the same feed, a 200 ms TCP stall is permanent — during those 200 ms, the price has moved 1000+ times, and the trading opportunity is gone.

Different workloads, different protocols. UDP for speed-critical broadcasts. TCP for correctness-critical orders. The industry knows this — we'll see exactly how in Part 4.

04 · Industry Practice

The Industry's Answer — split the protocol

┌─────────────────────────────┐ │ Exchange Matching Engine │ └────────────┬────────────────┘ │ ┌─────┴─────┐ │ │ Market Data Order Entry (broadcast) (per-client) │ │ UDP multicast TCP / FIX / OUCH (speed) (reliability)

The matching engine doesn't pick a single protocol. It picks two, by purpose.

UDP Multicast — Market Data

Same prices broadcast to every subscriber simultaneously. Loss is tolerable; head-of-line blocking is not.

TCP / FIX / OUCH — Order Entry

One order, sent once, must arrive. Reliability matters more than tail latency — and TCP's tail is rare when there's no broadcast contention.

Real exchanges don't pick TCP or UDP. They use both, by purpose.

04 · Industry Practice

The Clever Hybrid — CME's UDP + TCP recovery

CME's MDP 3.0 sends market data over UDP multicast with sequence numbers. Subscribers detect gaps and recover lost packets via a separate TCP recovery channel.

UDP MC stream: … 100, 101, [lost], 103, 104, 105 … ↑ subscriber sees gap ↓ TCP recovery query: "send me seq 102" TCP recovery reply: <seq 102 payload>

The result

Hot path stays on UDP — no HOL blocking.

Recovery happens out-of-band on TCP — used only when a gap is detected.

Subscribers without gaps pay nothing for reliability they don't need.

Best of both worlds — UDP speed, TCP recovery, no head-of-line blocking.

Source: CME Group, "MDP 3.0 Channel Recovery", official market data documentation.

05 · Conclusion

Conclusion & Future Directions

What we learned

  • HFT bottleneck is software — dproc + dqueue, not physics.
  • Kernel bypass (DPDK / OpenOnload / RDMA) attacks dproc.
  • Protocol split (UDP + TCP) attacks dqueue and tail latency.
  • Both layers must be optimized together — neither alone is enough.

Future directions

  • QUIC / SRT — user-space transports with built-in loss recovery.
  • SmartNICs / P4-programmable switches — pushing logic into the network fabric.
  • FPGA acceleration — tick-to-trade in hardware, at the cost of agility.
  • The next 5 years: how much of a trading strategy can fit inside the NIC itself?
1 μs is not just a number. It's the difference between a profitable trade and a missed one — and the industry has built an entire technology stack to chase it.
